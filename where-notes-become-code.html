How variety emerges

Parallel strategies, not equivalences
01A few building blocks▾
Raga space

Every Carnatic student begins with seven syllables, sa, ri, ga, ma, pa, da, ni: the swaras, the tones from which all melody is drawn.

Sequence space

The genetic code manages with even fewer, just four chemical letters, the nucleotide bases: adenine (A), thymine (T), guanine (G) and cytosine (C). Their order along DNA carries the instructions for life.

02A handful of rules▾
Raga space

Sa and Pa remain fixed, while each of the other five swaras takes one variant, a shade higher or lower. Every allowed combination yields one of 72 parent scales, the melakartas. The seventeenth-century scholar Venkatamakhi mapped all 72.

(Ri–Ga pairings: 6 ways · Da–Ni pairings: 6 ways · Ma: 2 ways → 6 × 6 × 2 = 72)
Sequence space

In the cell, the genetic code is read three letters at a time, so four letters give rise to exactly 64 three-letter words: the codons.

(4 choices for each of 3 positions → 4 × 4 × 4 = 4³ = 64)
03Where meaning lives▾
Raga space

“The raga does not lie in the notes, but in between the notes.” The in-between is the realm of gamakas: the oscillations, glides and microtonal inflections that bring each swara to life. Without them, even the most distinctive raga becomes unrecognisable. The notes are all there; the music is not.

Sequence space

Molecular biology has moved beyond seeing DNA as a fixed instruction manual. Gene regulation matters just as much: which genes are switched on or off, and how strongly. A liver cell and a nerve cell carry the same DNA, yet read it differently. Same notes; entirely different music.

04Repetition and the new▾
Raga space

A Carnatic phrase is rarely rendered in the same way twice. A singer offers it once, then returns to it, holding one note a little longer, bending another. These sequential elaborations, the sangatis, unfold the phrase’s potential without ever abandoning it.

Sequence space

The geneticist Susumu Ohno argued that repetition is evolution’s raw material. When a stretch of DNA is duplicated, one copy may keep its original function while the other accumulates changes and, over evolutionary time, may acquire a new one.

05A closed set, an open world▾
Raga space

From the 72 melakartas come the janya, or derived, ragas, which may leave out notes, change their order or add signature phrases. The generative space is practically limitless.

Sequence space

Of the 64 codons, 61 specify amino acids, the beads strung into proteins (the molecular machines that do most of a cell’s work); the other three mark where a protein ends. The generative space is practically limitless.

06Exploration within constraint▾
Raga space

In concert, a musician improvises in the moment: manodharma, the mind’s own creativity. No two renderings of a raga are the same, yet every phrase stays within its rules.

Sequence space

Across successive generations, living things vary, and the environment shapes which variations persist. All obey the chemistry of life.

A guide for the curious

Two wheels, two worlds

No music or biology needed. Each wheel shows a whole world built from a handful of pieces.

  1. Read from the centre outward. One wheel holds the notes of Carnatic music; the other, the letters of the genetic code.
  2. Hover, then tap. Hover a ring to name it; tap the outer band to open a card. Use ← and → to walk around the wheel.
  3. Tap the building blocks to hear them and light them up on the wheel. Every key works this way; ← and → step through.
  4. Listen. Each raga card links to a sung recording. The speaker button plays each scale or codon as you step.
  5. Look below for the colour keys, and for six strategies the two worlds share: tap a number, then use the arrow keys.

Nothing here needs to be memorised. Follow your curiosity.
